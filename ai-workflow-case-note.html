How I Use AI to Design
Better Content, Faster

A documented workflow showing how I integrate AI tools into instructional design — with the prompt strategy, human review checkpoints, and time math behind it.

Primary Tools Claude · Perplexity · Synthesia
Applied To eLearning + Assessment
Time Savings ~40% per module

01 · Context

The problem with "I use AI tools."

Most L&D professionals say they use AI. Few have a documented, repeatable process for how. The difference matters — both for quality control and for what you can actually teach others to do.

This case note documents the AI-assisted instructional design workflow I've developed across projects at Inteleos, including the Global CHIVA Program and internal eLearning builds. It is not a tool list. It is a process — with defined inputs, deliberate prompting, and human judgment at every critical gate.

Principle: AI accelerates the work I already know how to do. It does not replace the instructional design expertise behind it. Every output is reviewed, reshaped, and owned by me before it touches a learner.

02 · The Workflow

Six stages. Defined inputs and outputs at each.

1
Content intake and context-setting

Before touching any AI tool, I define the learning objective, audience, and constraints. I pull in raw SME content — slide decks, clinical notes, recorded meetings — and create a brief that anchors the entire build. This stage is human-only.

Learning objective framework SME source materials Audience analysis
Time: 30–60 min · No AI
2
Research and source verification

I use Perplexity Enterprise to rapidly surface supporting evidence, current clinical or domain references, and fill knowledge gaps in SME-provided material. Every claim that will inform learner-facing content gets verified here before it moves forward.

Perplexity Enterprise Source verification checklist
Time: 20–45 min · ~60% AI-assisted
3
Content drafting with structured prompts

I use Claude to draft module content — narration scripts, scenario setups, and learning checks — using a layered prompting approach. I do not use a single "write me a module" prompt. Each component gets its own focused input, with the learning objective and audience context baked in.

Claude Prompt template library Objective-aligned framing
Time: 45–90 min · ~70% AI-drafted, 30% ID-shaped
4
Assessment development

MCQs and case-based items are drafted with AI support, then reviewed and revised against Bloom's taxonomy alignment, distractor quality, and item writing standards. Assessment review is a non-negotiable human gate — no AI-generated item ships without full editorial review.

Claude Item review rubric Bloom's alignment check
Time: 30–60 min · 50% AI draft / 50% human review
5
Media production

Approved scripts go into Synthesia for AI avatar video or Vyond for animated explainers, depending on the content type. I configure presenters, pacing, and visual elements to match brand and learner context. AI generates the media; I direct it.

Synthesia Vyond Brand and tone guide
Time: 30–45 min · ~80% AI-generated
6
LMS build and QA

Finished content goes into TalentLMS or Articulate Rise. I complete a final QA pass — checking objective alignment, learner flow, accessibility, and assessment logic — before any module is published. This stage is human-only.

TalentLMS Articulate Rise / Storyline QA checklist
Time: 45–60 min · No AI

03 · Prompt Strategy

How I prompt. Not what the tool does.

The quality of AI-assisted content is a direct function of the quality of the prompt. I use a layered structure that grounds every request in instructional design logic — not just content requests.

Example — narration script prompt structure

Role: You are an instructional designer working with clinical SMEs.

Objective: Write a 90-second narration script for a module on [topic].

Audience: [credential level, prior knowledge, clinical context].

Constraints: Plain language. No jargon without definition. Active voice. No more than 3 concepts per segment.

Tone: Authoritative but accessible. Not lecture-like.

Output format: Script only — no headers, no meta-commentary.

Each constraint maps to an instructional design principle, not a stylistic preference.

I maintain a small prompt library — roughly 12 templates — organized by content type (narration, scenario, MCQ stem, distractor, learning objective). Each template has been iterated over multiple projects and reflects what actually produces usable first drafts.

04 · Human Review Gates

Where I always intervene — and why.

AI-generated content has predictable failure modes in L&D: it flattens nuance, loses audience awareness, and produces assessment items that look correct but measure the wrong thing. These are the four points where I always intervene, regardless of how good the draft looks.

Objective alignment check

Does every content element trace back to a stated learning objective? AI drafts often add "interesting" content that doesn't serve the learning goal.

Audience calibration

Is the language, example selection, and assumed prior knowledge appropriate for this specific learner? Generic outputs require clinical or contextual adjustment.

Assessment review

Every MCQ item is reviewed against item writing standards: stem clarity, distractor plausibility, single correct answer, absence of cueing language.

Factual verification

Any clinical, technical, or domain-specific claim is verified against primary sources before it appears in learner-facing content. No exceptions.

05 · Before and After

What changed when I systematized this.

Before — traditional ID workflow
  • 2–3 hours to produce a first-draft script
  • SME content required heavy editing before it was usable
  • Video production was an external dependency
  • Assessment items written entirely from scratch each time
  • Limited iteration — one draft, then SME review
After — AI-assisted workflow
  • 45–60 min to a reviewed, usable first draft
  • AI structures raw SME content before human editing begins
  • Video produced internally with Synthesia or Vyond
  • Assessment item drafts generated, then human-reviewed
  • Multiple iteration cycles possible within the same session

06 · Results

The numbers — and what they mean.

40% Reduction in eLearning production time per module
90% Learner satisfaction rate maintained across AI-assisted programs
12+ Reusable prompt templates built and actively maintained

The 40% time savings isn't from cutting corners. It comes from eliminating the low-value parts of the process — blank-page drafting, manual reformatting, and waiting for external media production — so more time goes to the high-value parts: SME relationships, quality review, and instructional alignment. That's the actual goal of AI integration in L&D.

AI didn't change what good instructional design looks like. It changed how long it takes to get there.
